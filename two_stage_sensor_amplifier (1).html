Two-Stage Sensor Amplifier Using Op-Amp

Two cascaded non-inverting amplifiers (10 × 10 = 100) that raise a 10 mV peak, 1 kHz sensor signal to 1 V peak. Includes derivations, an interactive calculator, waveforms, Multisim procedure, solved problems, quiz and GATE/interview preparation.

Learning objectives

  • Derive and apply the non-inverting gain Av = 1 + Rf/Rg.
  • Calculate stage-wise and overall gain, peak, peak-to-peak and RMS voltages of a cascade.
  • Build, measure and troubleshoot the circuit in Multisim, and compare measurements with theory.
  • Recognise practical limits: saturation, bandwidth, slew rate, noise, offset.

1Introduction and Learning Objectives

What is a two-stage sensor amplifier?

A two-stage sensor amplifier connects two op-amp amplifier stages in cascade: the output of the first stage is the input of the second. Here both stages are non-inverting amplifiers with a gain of 10, giving an overall voltage gain of 10 × 10 = 100.

Why amplify low-amplitude sensor signals?

Many sensors, especially biomedical ones, produce very small voltages. A data converter (ADC) or a display usually needs signals of the order of volts. Amplification brings the signal well above the noise of later stages and into the useful input range of the ADC.

Signal / sensorTypical amplitude (order of magnitude)
ECG (heart)about 0.5 – 4 mV
EMG (muscle)about 0.1 – 5 mV
EEG (brain)about 10 – 100 µV
Thermocoupletens of µV per °C
Typical ADC full-scale inputabout 1 – 5 V

Values are approximate and vary with electrode, subject and sensor.

Importance and applications

Signal conditioning (amplify, filter, level-shift) is the first block of any measurement chain: biomedical instrumentation (ECG, EMG, pulse sensors), temperature and strain sensing, data acquisition, and audio pre-amplification.

Prerequisite concepts

  • Ideal op-amp: infinite open-loop gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance.
  • Virtual short (V+ ≈ V−) under negative feedback.
  • Ohm's law and voltage divider.
  • Sine-wave peak, peak-to-peak and RMS values.
  • Decibel gain: 20 log10(A).

Important terminology

  • Voltage gain (Av): Vout/Vin, dimensionless.
  • Cascade connection: output of one stage drives the next; gains multiply.
  • Non-inverting amplifier: input at the + terminal; output in phase with input.
  • Feedback resistor (Rf): connects output to the inverting input.
  • Input / output voltage: Vin, Vout measured to ground.
  • Saturation: output stuck near a supply rail; it cannot rise further.
  • Distortion: output waveform shape differs from the scaled input (e.g. clipping).

Advantages

  • High gain from simple parts
  • Very high input impedance (non-inverting)
  • Gain set accurately by two resistors
  • Gain split over two stages keeps bandwidth higher

Limitations

  • Noise and offset are amplified too
  • Finite bandwidth and slew rate
  • Output limited by supply rails
  • Not a full medical-grade instrumentation amplifier

3Working Principle

  1. The sensor produces a small 10 mV peak sine wave at 1 kHz.
  2. The first non-inverting amplifier (U1) multiplies it by 10.
  3. Stage-1 output is about 100 mV peak.
  4. This output is connected directly to the + input of the second stage (U2).
  5. U2 multiplies it by 10 again.
  6. The final theoretical output is about 1 V peak (gain 100).

Role of the terminals and feedback

  • Non-inverting input (+): receives the signal. Because the input goes to the + terminal, a rising input makes the output rise: output and input are in phase (0°).
  • Inverting input (−): receives a fraction of the output through the Rf–Rg divider: V− = Vout·Rg/(Rg+Rf) = 0.1·Vout.
  • Negative feedback: the op-amp changes Vout until V− ≈ V+ (virtual short). So Vout = Vin/β, i.e. gain = 1/β = 10.
  • Feedback resistor Rf: larger Rf (relative to Rg) means less feedback and more gain.

Practical limitations

Bandwidth

The gain–bandwidth product (GBW) is roughly constant: bandwidth ≈ GBW / closed-loop gain. Example: GBW = 1 MHz and gain 10 gives about 100 kHz per stage. Two cascaded stages together have a slightly lower −3 dB frequency (about 0.64 × 100 kHz ≈ 64 kHz).

Slew rate

The output cannot change faster than the slew rate. For a sine of peak Vp at frequency f, the maximum slope is 2πfVp. For 1 V peak at 1 kHz this is 6.28 V/ms = 6.28 mV/µs, which is easy for almost any op-amp.

Output swing & supply

The output cannot exceed the supply rails; real op-amps typically stop a volt or two below them. With ±15 V rails, a 1 V output is far from saturation, but a larger input would clip.

Distortion

Clipping (saturation), slew-rate limiting and excessive frequency all distort the waveform. Noise and input offset of stage 1 are amplified by both stages.

Multisim note: OPAMP_5T_VIRTUAL is a simplified virtual model. Its actual open-loop gain, bandwidth and output swing are set by the model in the component properties. Read those parameters instead of assuming them, and always check your measurements against theory.

4Mathematical Equations and Derivation

Derivation of the non-inverting gain

Assume an ideal op-amp with negative feedback: no current enters either input, and V− = V+ = Vin.

  1. Current through Rg (to ground): I = Vin / Rg.
  2. No current enters the − input, so the same current I flows through Rf.
  3. Output voltage: Vout = V− + I·Rf = Vin + (Vin/Rg)·Rf.
Av = Vout/Vin = 1 + (Rf / Rg)
  • Av = voltage gain (dimensionless)
  • Rf = feedback resistance in ohms (Ω), from output to inverting input
  • Rg = resistance from inverting input to ground, in ohms (Ω)
Finite open-loop gain A: Av = A / (1 + Aβ), with β = Rg/(Rg+Rf). When Aβ >> 1 this reduces to 1/β = 1 + Rf/Rg.

First stage (U1)

Vin = 10 mV peak, Rf = R2 = 90 kΩ, Rg = R1 = 10 kΩ

Av1 = 1 + 90/10 = 10
V1(output) = 10 mV × 10 = 100 mV peak

Second stage (U2)

Input = 100 mV peak, Rf = R4 = 90 kΩ, Rg = R3 = 10 kΩ

Av2 = 1 + 90/10 = 10
Vout = 100 mV × 10 = 1000 mV = 1 V peak

Overall gain

Atotal = Av1 × Av2 = 10 × 10 = 100
Atotal = Vout / Vin = 1 V / 0.01 V = 100
In decibels: 20 log10(10) = 20 dB per stage; 20 log10(100) = 40 dB total

Peak, peak-to-peak and RMS

  • Peak (Vp): maximum value from zero to the top of the sine.
  • Peak-to-peak (Vpp): from the negative peak to the positive peak: Vpp = 2Vp.
  • RMS: the equivalent DC heating value: Vrms = Vp/√2 ≈ 0.707 Vp.

7Multisim Simulation Procedure

  1. Place two op-amps: Place → Component, group Analog, family ANALOG_VIRTUAL, choose OPAMP_5T_VIRTUAL (twice). Its five pins are +input, −input, output, V+ and V−.
  2. Add four resistors: R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 90 kΩ, R3 = 10 kΩ, R4 = 90 kΩ.
  3. Add the signal source: an AC voltage source. Set Voltage (Pk) = 10 mV and Frequency = 1 kHz. Check whether your source field is peak or RMS; an RMS setting of 10 mV would give 14.1 mV peak.
  4. Wire the cascade: source → U1 + input; U1 output → U2 + input.
  5. Connect supplies: two DC sources of 15 V in series with their junction grounded gives +15 V and −15 V. Connect +15 V to V+ and −15 V to V− of both op-amps.
  6. Feedback and ground resistors: R2 from U1 output to U1 − input; R1 from U1 − input to ground. R4 from U2 output to U2 − input; R3 from U2 − input to ground.
  7. Oscilloscope: in the screenshot the standard two-channel XSC1 is used: channel A on node 1 (input) and channel B on node 5 (final output), with the scope grounded. To see the stage-1 output (node 2) as well, use the four-channel oscilloscope (Simulate → Instruments) or move a probe to node 2.
  8. Run the simulation (Simulate → Run) and let it settle for a few cycles.
  9. Measure amplitude and period on each channel using the cursors; compute frequency = 1/period.
  10. Compare with theory (10 mV, 100 mV, 1 V peak; 1 kHz) using the comparison tool in Section 6. Do not assume the output is right until you have checked it.

8Solved Numerical Problems

Example 1 (Easy): gain of one non-inverting stage

Question: A non-inverting amplifier has Rf = 47 kΩ and Rg = 10 kΩ. Find its voltage gain.

Required: Av. Formula: Av = 1 + Rf/Rg.

Av = 1 + 47/10 = 1 + 4.7 = 5.7

Answer: Av = 5.7 (dimensionless), about 15.1 dB.

Example 2 (Medium): overall gain and output of two cascaded stages

Question: Vin = 5 mV peak. Stage 1: Rf1 = 90 kΩ, Rg1 = 10 kΩ. Stage 2: Rf2 = 19 kΩ, Rg2 = 1 kΩ. Find Atotal and Vout.

Required: Av1, Av2, Atotal, Vout. Formulas: Av = 1 + Rf/Rg; Atotal = Av1Av2.

Av1 = 1 + 90/10 = 10
Av2 = 1 + 19/1 = 20
Atotal = 10 × 20 = 200
Vout = 5 mV × 200 = 1000 mV

Answer: Atotal = 200; Vout = 1 V peak (stage-1 output = 50 mV peak).

Example 3 (Design): resistor values for a specified overall gain

Question: Design a two-stage non-inverting amplifier with an overall gain of 400, using equal gain in each stage.

Required: Rf and Rg. Formulas: Atotal = A1A2; A = 1 + Rf/Rg.

Equal stages: A2 = 400 ⇒ A = 20
1 + Rf/Rg = 20 ⇒ Rf/Rg = 19
Choose Rg = 1 kΩ ⇒ Rf = 19 kΩ

Answer: each stage Rg = 1 kΩ, Rf = 19 kΩ (nearest E96 value 19.1 kΩ gives A = 20.1 per stage, about 404 overall). Equal stage gains give the widest overall bandwidth for a fixed total gain.

Example 4 (Practical): saturation limited by the supply

Question: The same circuit (10 × 10) is run from ±15 V, and the op-amp output can swing only up to about ±13.5 V (assumed). The input is 200 mV peak sine. Find the stage-1 output, the final output, and the largest input that avoids clipping.

Required: V1(output), Vout, Vin(max). Formula: Vout = AtotalVin, limited to ±Vsat.

V1(output) = 200 mV × 10 = 2 V (below 13.5 V, so not clipped)
Ideal Vout = 2 V × 10 = 20 V > 13.5 V ⇒ clipped
Vin(max) = Vsat/Atotal = 13.5 V / 100 = 135 mV

Answer: stage 1 gives 2 V peak; the final output clips at about 13.5 V peak (flat-topped waveform, not 20 V). The largest undistorted input is about 135 mV peak.

Example 5 (Practical): slew-rate check

Question: What minimum slew rate is needed to output a 1 V peak, 1 kHz sine without slew limiting?

Formula: SRmin = 2πfVp.

SRmin = 2π × 1000 × 1 = 6283 V/s ≈ 6.28 mV/µs

Answer: about 6.28 mV/µs (0.00628 V/µs), far below the slew rate of typical op-amps (of the order of V/µs).

10GATE and Interview Preparation

GATE-style conceptual questions

G1. An op-amp has open-loop gain A = 104. It is used as a non-inverting amplifier with Rf = 90 kΩ, Rg = 10 kΩ. Find the closed-loop gain.
β = 10/(10+90) = 0.1. Af = A/(1 + Aβ) = 10000/1001 ≈ 9.99, i.e. about 0.1% below the ideal 10.
G2. Two cascaded stages have gains of 10 and 10. Express the overall gain in dB.
20 log 10 = 20 dB per stage; gains in dB add: 40 dB (equal to 20 log 100).
G3. Each stage (gain 10) is built with an op-amp of GBW = 1 MHz. Estimate the overall −3 dB bandwidth of the cascade.
Each stage: f1 = 1 MHz/10 = 100 kHz. For two identical single-pole stages, [1/(1+x2)] = 1/√2 requires (1+x2)2 = 2, so x = √(√2 − 1) = 0.6436. Overall f−3dB ≈ 64.4 kHz.
G4. Input 150 mV peak is applied to the 10 × 10 cascade. The output can swing only to ±13 V. What is the output peak?
Ideal output = 150 mV × 100 = 15 V > 13 V, so the output clips at about 13 V peak (flat-topped). Stage 1 gives 1.5 V and is not clipped.
G5. Which is correct for an ideal non-inverting amplifier: input resistance and output resistance?
Input resistance → infinite; output resistance → zero. (Real devices: very high and very low.) This is why stage 2 does not load stage 1.
G6. For a fixed total gain, why is it better to put more gain in the first stage?
Noise added by stage 2 is divided by the gain of stage 1 when referred to the input, so a high-gain first stage lowers total input-referred noise (as in the cascaded noise formula). But very high stage-1 gain raises the risk of saturation from offsets and reduces its bandwidth, so a balance is used.

Core electronics interview questions

Why use a non-inverting amplifier for a sensor?
Its input impedance is very high, so it does not load a high-impedance sensor, and the output stays in phase.
Why use two stages of gain 10 instead of one stage of gain 100?
With a 1 MHz GBW, one stage of gain 100 has about 10 kHz bandwidth, while two stages of gain 10 give about 64 kHz overall. Resistor ratios stay moderate and each stage has more feedback (better accuracy and lower distortion).
What is the virtual short?
With negative feedback and very high open-loop gain, the differential input voltage is nearly zero, so V− ≈ V+, though no current flows between them.
What happens if the feedback resistor is removed?
The op-amp runs open-loop with huge gain; it behaves like a comparator and saturates at a supply rail for even a tiny input.
What does input offset voltage do in this circuit?
It is amplified by the full gain. For example 1 mV of offset gives about 100 mV of DC error at the output of a gain-100 cascade (more offset from stage 1 than stage 2).
How does resistor tolerance affect the gain?
The gain depends on the ratio Rf/Rg. With 1% resistors, worst case per stage is about ±1.8% (gain 10.18 instead of 10), so about ±3.6% overall.
How can noise be reduced?
Use low-noise op-amps and lower resistor values, add filtering to limit bandwidth, shield and ground properly, and give the first stage adequate gain.
How does this differ from an instrumentation amplifier?
An instrumentation amplifier has differential inputs with very high CMRR and a gain set by one resistor. This circuit is single-ended and has no common-mode rejection beyond the op-amp's own.

Common mistakes in two-stage amplifier calculations

  • Using A = Rf/Rg = 9 and forgetting the “1 +”.
  • Adding stage gains (10 + 10 = 20) instead of multiplying (100). Only decibel values add.
  • Using the inverting formula −Rf/Rg; the non-inverting output is not inverted.
  • Mixing peak, peak-to-peak and RMS (for example reporting 1 V peak as 1 V RMS).
  • Ignoring saturation and clipping when the ideal output exceeds the supply.
  • Setting the Multisim source in RMS instead of peak.
  • Swapping Rf and Rg, or connecting feedback to the + input.
  • Assuming ideal results without checking bandwidth, slew rate and offset.

Viva questions and answers

What is the gain of each stage here?
10, since 1 + 90/10 = 10.
What is the final output for a 10 mV peak input?
1 V peak (2 V peak-to-peak, 0.707 V RMS).
What is the phase between input and output?
0°: the output is in phase because the signal goes to the non-inverting input.
Why is the −15 V supply needed?
A dual supply lets the output swing both positive and negative around ground for an AC signal.
What would you check if the Multisim output is 100 mV instead of 1 V?
One stage is acting with gain 1: check that R3 (or R1) is connected to ground and R4 (or R2) is not shorted, then measure each stage separately.
Which waveform parameter is shown by the scope cursor reading from zero to the top of the sine?
The peak value; the top-to-bottom reading is the peak-to-peak value.
Does the second stage affect the first?
Practically no: the non-inverting input of U2 has very high impedance and U1 has very low output impedance.
Is this a medical-grade ECG amplifier?
No. It lacks differential input, high CMRR, patient isolation, protection and filtering that a real biomedical front end requires.

11Advantages, Limitations and Applications

Advantages

  • High overall voltage gain (100) from two simple stages.
  • Suitable for low-amplitude sensor signals: brings millivolts up to a usable volt-level.
  • High input impedance of the non-inverting configuration, so the sensor is barely loaded.
  • Negative feedback makes the gain set by resistors, improving accuracy and reducing distortion.

Limitations

  • Noise accumulation: noise and offset of stage 1 are amplified by both stages.
  • Bandwidth and gain limits: gain × bandwidth is limited by the op-amp GBW.
  • Output saturation and distortion when the ideal output exceeds the rails.
  • Proper negative feedback is essential; a wrong connection ruins the gain or causes saturation.

Applications

  • Biomedical signal conditioning: ECG, EMG and pulse-sensor front ends (before filtering and ADC).
  • Instrumentation: thermocouple, strain-gauge and photodiode signal amplification.
  • Data-acquisition front ends and audio pre-amplifiers.
Important: a simple two-stage voltage amplifier is not automatically a complete medical-grade instrumentation amplifier. A real biomedical front end also needs a differential instrumentation amplifier with high CMRR, input protection, filtering, low noise, and patient safety/isolation.
